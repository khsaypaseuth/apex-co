Companies operating in Laos are generally subject to tax on their profits — commonly referred to as profit tax or corporate income tax (CIT). This article explains the concept and the compliance rhythm in plain language. The only figure it quotes is the standard headline rate, dated to the time of writing, because rates and incentives are set by law and change; the current position must be confirmed with the tax authority.

> **Needs legal verification before publication.**

## What is taxed, in principle

Corporate income tax applies to a company's taxable profit — broadly, business income minus the expenses the tax rules allow to be deducted. Two points matter in practice:

- **Accounting profit and taxable profit are not the same thing.** The tax rules define which expenses are deductible, how assets are depreciated, and how losses are treated. Adjustments between the accounts and the tax return are normal.
- **Documentation drives deductibility.** Expenses generally need proper supporting documents to be deductible; undocumented spending tends to be added back.

## Who it applies to

- Lao companies, on the profits of their business
- Foreign-invested companies operating in Laos
- Branches of foreign companies, on their Lao operations
- Certain sectors and promoted investments may benefit from incentives or special regimes under investment legislation — whether one applies to you should be verified rather than assumed

Small enterprises may fall under simplified or presumptive regimes rather than full profit tax accounting, depending on scale; which regime applies should be confirmed with the tax authority.

## The compliance cycle

| Stage | What it generally involves |
| --- | --- |
| During the year | Keeping accounts to Lao requirements; making any interim payments the rules require |
| Year end | Preparing financial statements for the accounting year |
| Finalisation | Preparing the annual tax return, adjusting accounting profit to taxable profit |
| Filing and payment | Filing and settling by the deadlines set by the tax authority |

Under the rules in force as of July 2026, profit tax is declared on a twice-yearly cycle, with payments made in advance and settled through the annual finalisation. The specific deadlines and the advance payment schedule are set by regulation and should be confirmed for the current year.

## Common mistakes

- **Weak bookkeeping all year, heroics at year end.** The annual return is only as good as the records behind it.
- **Assuming every business expense is deductible.** Deductibility follows the tax rules, not intuition; personal or undocumented expenses are common adjustment points.
- **Forgetting interim obligations.** Where advance payments apply, missing them creates penalties even if the annual position is correct.
- **Relying on incentives that were never formally granted.** Investment incentives generally require the right approvals and paperwork — a verbal understanding is not a tax position.

## Rates and incentives

Profit tax rates, incentive regimes, and small-business thresholds are set by law and have changed over time. As of July 2026, the standard profit tax rate for most companies is 20 percent under the Income Tax Law No. 67/NA (2019), with different rates for certain sectors — higher for areas such as minerals and tobacco, lower for some promoted activities — and lump-sum regimes for the smallest businesses. Because rates and incentives change, the current position and any incentives relevant to your sector should be confirmed with the tax authority or a qualified adviser before you build them into a business plan.
