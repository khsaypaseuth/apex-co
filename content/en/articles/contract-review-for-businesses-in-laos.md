Contracts are where business risk actually lives. Leases, supply agreements, service contracts, employment contracts, shareholder agreements — each one allocates money, obligations, and risk between the parties, and each one will be read most carefully on the day something goes wrong. Contract review is the discipline of reading them that carefully *before* signing. For businesses in Laos, review also means checking that the contract works under Lao law and in the Lao operating environment, not just on paper.

## Why review matters more than templates

Templates and copied contracts are everywhere, and they fail in predictable ways:

- They were drafted for a different legal system, so clauses may not operate as intended under Lao law
- They describe a deal that is not quite your deal, leaving your actual expectations undocumented
- They are silent on the scenarios that matter locally — currency, language, approvals, and enforcement

A review does not need to make a contract longer. It needs to make the contract match reality.

## What a good review covers

| Area | Questions a reviewer asks |
| --- | --- |
| Parties | Is each party correctly named, registered, and represented by someone with authority to sign? |
| Scope and price | Is what is being delivered, by when, and for how much, stated without ambiguity? |
| Payment terms | Currency, invoicing, timing, and what happens on late payment |
| Term and termination | How long it runs, how each side can exit, and what survives termination |
| Risk allocation | Warranties, liability limits, indemnities, and force majeure |
| Language | Which language version controls if the contract exists in more than one |
| Governing law and disputes | Which law applies and where disputes are resolved |
| Compliance fit | Whether the deal requires licences, registrations, or approvals to be lawful |

## Points that deserve special attention in Laos

- **Language versions.** Contracts with Lao parties or authorities often exist in Lao and another language. Decide explicitly which version prevails — silence invites disputes.
- **Authority to sign.** Check that the signatory can actually bind the company; a signature from the wrong person may leave you without a counterparty.
- **Registration and approval requirements.** Some contract types or transactions may need registration or official approval to be fully effective; whether yours does should be confirmed for the specific contract type.
- **Enforcement realism.** A beautifully drafted dispute clause is only useful if it points somewhere you can realistically enforce. Think about where the counterparty's assets are.
- **Employment contracts** interact with Lao labour rules, which set minimum standards a contract cannot sign away.

## Common mistakes

- Signing the counterparty's first draft unchanged, on the assumption it is "standard"
- Leaving key commercial terms in emails and messages instead of the contract
- Copying a contract from another deal and missing the clauses that no longer fit
- Reviewing only new contracts and never revisiting long-running ones after renewals or scope changes

## When to have a contract reviewed

Before signing is best. Beyond that, sensible trigger points include: entering a new market or activity, renewing or extending a significant agreement, changes in ownership on either side, and any point where the relationship has already shown friction.
