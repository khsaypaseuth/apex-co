This guide is for couples and individuals building a long-term life in the Lao PDR — particularly marriages between Lao and foreign nationals, and the residence arrangements that follow. It connects the family-law side (getting the marriage legally registered) with the immigration side (visas and stay permits based on marriage or other long-term grounds), because in practice the two depend on each other. Document requirements and procedures vary by nationality and district and change over time, so confirm the current requirements for your case before starting.

## Who this guide is for

- Lao–foreign couples planning to marry in Laos
- Couples already married abroad who need the marriage recognised in Laos
- Foreign spouses arranging visas and stay permits based on marriage
- Foreign nationals exploring other long-term stay routes — work, investment, retirement-style arrangements

## Chapter 1 — Getting legally married in Laos

A ceremony does not create a legal marriage — registration does. This chapter walks through the path for mixed-nationality couples: the application for permission to marry, the documents the foreign spouse needs from home (identity, single-status confirmation, background documents), translation and legalisation so Lao authorities accept them, interviews and checks, and finally registration and the marriage certificate. It flags the common failure point: foreign documents that arrive too old, untranslated, or unauthenticated.

## Chapter 2 — Married abroad: recognition in Laos

Many couples marry in the foreign spouse's country or a third country. This chapter explains why a foreign marriage certificate may need recognition or recording in Laos before it supports visa applications, property matters, or children's documents — and the typical legalisation-and-translation chain involved.

## Chapter 3 — The spouse's immigration status

Marriage does not automatically confer the right to live in Laos. This chapter covers the visa and stay permit arrangements commonly used by foreign spouses, how the registered marriage supports the application, renewal rhythms, and what happens to status if circumstances change. It also addresses the common question of whether a spouse may work — living rights and working rights are separate, and work typically requires its own permit.

## Chapter 4 — Children and family documents

Birth registration, nationality questions for children of mixed marriages, passports, and the school-and-daily-life paperwork families need. This chapter emphasises doing documents in the right order and keeping certified copies, because each document builds on the previous ones.

## Chapter 5 — Property, money, and planning as a mixed couple

Foreign spouses face restrictions on land ownership in Laos, which shapes how couples structure homes and assets. This chapter outlines, in general terms, how marital property works under Lao family law, the particular care needed around land held in the Lao spouse's name, and why couples should take advice before major purchases rather than after. It is deliberately general — property structuring is fact-specific and needs individual advice.

## Chapter 6 — Other long-term stay routes

Marriage is one basis for long-term residence; work, investment, and business ownership are others. This chapter briefly maps the alternatives and their trade-offs, for readers whose long-term stay does not run through marriage, and links to the dedicated articles on each route.

## Chapter 7 — Keeping everything current

Long-term life in Laos is a rhythm of renewals: stay permits, passports, and the records that link the family together. The closing chapter provides a simple family document register and renewal calendar, and lists the life events — a move, a new child, a change of employment — that trigger paperwork.
