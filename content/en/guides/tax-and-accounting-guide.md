This guide explains how the Lao tax and accounting system works for companies, in plain language: which taxes apply, when filings are due, what books must be kept, and how the year closes. It is written for business owners and managers rather than accountants, and it deliberately avoids quoting rates, thresholds, and deadlines as fixed facts — these are set by regulation, change through amendments, and should be confirmed with the tax authority or a qualified adviser for your situation.

> **Needs legal verification before publication.**

## Who this guide is for

- Founders and managers of new Lao companies setting up their first compliance routine
- Foreign-invested companies aligning group practices with Lao requirements
- Owners of existing businesses who suspect their books and filings have drifted
- Anyone who wants to understand what their accountant should be doing

## Chapter 1 — The Lao tax system at a glance

The main taxes an operating company encounters: profit (corporate income) tax, value added tax, withholding obligations, and payroll-related contributions. This chapter explains what each tax is for, who administers it, and how the pieces relate — including the principle that obligations begin when the company is registered, not when it becomes profitable.

## Chapter 2 — Registration: getting into the system properly

Tax registration and the taxpayer identification number, VAT registration where the criteria are met, and social security registration for employees. This chapter covers the order these typically happen in after enterprise registration, what documents the authorities commonly request, and the problems that follow when a company trades before its registrations are complete.

## Chapter 3 — The filing calendar

Lao companies file on a recurring rhythm — commonly monthly or quarterly declarations depending on the tax, plus an annual finalisation. This chapter shows how to build a compliance calendar, why nil filings are required even in inactive months, and how penalties for late or missed filings typically accumulate quietly until a certificate or bank process forces the issue. Specific deadlines vary and should be confirmed against current regulations.

> **Needs legal verification before publication.**

## Chapter 4 — Books, records, and invoices

Lao accounting rules require companies to keep proper books in a compliant form, retain supporting documents, and use invoices correctly — VAT-registered businesses in particular must handle tax invoices properly. This chapter explains what a compliant record set looks like in practice, the common gap between "we have receipts somewhere" and auditable books, and why mixing personal and company money is the root of most year-end problems.

## Chapter 5 — Payroll, withholding, and social security

Employing staff brings payroll obligations: calculating and withholding personal income tax on salaries, remitting employer and employee social security contributions, and keeping employment records aligned with what is declared. This chapter walks through the monthly payroll cycle and the common mistakes — unregistered staff, allowances handled inconsistently, and withholding on contractor payments overlooked.

## Chapter 6 — Year-end: finalisation and financial statements

The year closes with reconciled books, financial statements, and the annual profit tax finalisation. This chapter explains what the finalisation involves, why it goes badly when monthly filings and the books tell different stories, and how audit or inspection interactions typically proceed. It ends with a checklist for arriving at year-end with no surprises.

## Chapter 7 — When to get help

Signs that a business has outgrown do-it-yourself compliance: growing staff, VAT registration, foreign shareholders needing group reporting, or a backlog of unfiled periods. This chapter describes what professional bookkeeping and tax support should deliver, and how catching up on a backlog is typically approached.
