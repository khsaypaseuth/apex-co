This guide walks through the full journey of starting a business in the Lao PDR — from the first planning decisions to the end of the first compliant year of operation. It is written in plain language for people who have not set up a Lao company before, and it deliberately avoids quoting fees, capital figures, or processing times, because those change and should be confirmed for your specific case at the time you file.

> **Needs legal verification before publication.**

## Who this guide is for

- Lao entrepreneurs formalising a business for the first time
- Foreign investors setting up a wholly foreign-owned company or a joint venture
- Companies abroad opening a Lao branch or representative office
- Anyone advising or partnering with the above

## Chapter 1 — Before you register: the decisions that shape everything

The most expensive mistakes in Lao company setup happen before any form is filed. This chapter covers the questions to settle first: what exactly the business will do (the registered activity determines which licenses and restrictions apply), whether the activity is open to foreign ownership and under what conditions, who the shareholders and directors will be, and where the business will be registered. It also explains why copying another company's structure is risky — conditions attach to activities, not to templates.

## Chapter 2 — Choosing a legal structure

Laos offers several enterprise forms, and most businesses choose between a sole trader registration and a limited company; foreign companies may also consider a branch or representative office. This chapter compares them in practical terms — liability, ownership flexibility, credibility with banks and partners, and the compliance load each carries — and explains why the limited company is the common default for foreign-owned businesses while a representative office cannot conduct revenue-earning activity.

## Chapter 3 — Enterprise registration step by step

The core registration process typically runs through name reservation and approval, preparation of founding documents (articles of association, shareholder documents, office address evidence), filing with the enterprise registration office, and issuance of the enterprise registration certificate. This chapter describes each step, what the authority commonly asks for, and how foreign shareholder documents are handled — translation and legalisation are frequent causes of delay. Timeframes and fees vary by case and are not quoted here; confirm them with the registration office when you file.

## Chapter 4 — Licenses and permits after registration

The registration certificate creates the company; it does not authorise every activity. Many activities require an operating license from the sector ministry — and some require approval before registration. This chapter explains how to identify the licenses your activity requires, the common pattern of sector approvals, and signage, address, and other practical requirements that inspections commonly check.

## Chapter 5 — Tax registration and the accounting setup

Tax obligations begin when the company exists, not when it earns. This chapter covers the taxpayer identification number — which, as of July 2026, is issued together with the enterprise registration certificate — VAT registration where criteria are met, the filing calendar a new company should diary from day one, social security registration for staff, and setting up books that comply with Lao accounting requirements. It also explains why nil filings matter — dormant companies that file nothing commonly accumulate penalties.

## Chapter 6 — Banking, capital, and premises

Opening the company bank account, documenting the injection of registered capital (especially important for foreign investors, whose capital import should be properly evidenced), and putting the office lease in a form the authorities accept. Foreigners cannot own land in Laos, so this chapter also outlines how premises are typically secured through leases.

## Chapter 7 — The first year: staying compliant

The first year sets the pattern. This chapter provides a practical rhythm: periodic tax declarations, social security remittances, license renewals, reporting changes (address, shareholders, directors) to the registration authority, and the annual profit tax finalisation. It closes with the signs that it is time to bring in professional accounting support rather than improvising.
