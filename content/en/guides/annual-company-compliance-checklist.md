A Lao company's obligations do not end when the registration certificate is issued — they begin there. This guide is organised as a working checklist of what a company operating in the Lao PDR should keep current through the year, grouped by area, with the reasoning behind each item. Deadlines, fees, and renewal cycles vary by company and are revised by the authorities from time to time, so pair this checklist with confirmation of the current requirements for your specific case.

> **Needs legal verification before publication.**

## Who this guide is for

- Directors and managers responsible for keeping a Lao company in good standing
- Foreign shareholders who need visibility into what their Lao entity must do
- Office managers and accountants who own the compliance calendar
- Buyers or investors reviewing whether a target company is actually compliant

## Chapter 1 — Corporate registrations and licenses

The enterprise registration certificate and operating licenses are living documents. This chapter covers: checking which licenses carry renewal dates and diarising them, reporting changes to shareholders, directors, capital, address, or activities to the registration authority when they happen, and keeping certified copies of the company's core documents organised and accessible. Companies commonly discover an expired license only when a bank, customer, or inspector asks for it.

## Chapter 2 — The tax year

The recurring heart of compliance. This chapter lists the typical cycle: periodic tax declarations through the year (including nil filings in inactive periods), withholding remittances, VAT obligations for registered businesses, the annual profit tax finalisation, and keeping tax payment evidence filed. It also covers the annual interactions many companies have with the tax authority around certificates and confirmations, which typically require the filing history to be clean.

## Chapter 3 — Accounting records and financial statements

Books that comply with Lao accounting requirements, supporting documents retained and organised, bank statements reconciled, and annual financial statements prepared. This chapter explains the standard a company should hold itself to so that an audit, inspection, or due diligence exercise is an inconvenience rather than a crisis.

## Chapter 4 — Employees: labour and social security

Employment compliance is checked annually in practice even where filings are monthly. The checklist here: written contracts in place for all staff, social security registration and contributions current for every employee, payroll withholding aligned with declared salaries, leave and overtime records kept, and workplace policies consistent with the labour rules. For foreign staff, work permits and stay permits current, with renewals prepared before expiry.

## Chapter 5 — Immigration items for foreign shareholders and staff

Visas, stay permits, and work permits each carry their own expiry dates, and family members' documents are commonly linked to the principal's. This chapter provides a simple register format for tracking everyone's documents and lead times for renewals, and explains why gaps are far more expensive to repair than to prevent.

## Chapter 6 — Contracts, insurance, and corporate housekeeping

The annual review items that fall outside tax and licensing: key contracts approaching expiry or auto-renewal, lease terms and registered address alignment, trademark renewals where the company holds registrations, insurance coverage, and minutes or resolutions recording the year's significant decisions. Small housekeeping now prevents disputes later.

## Chapter 7 — Building the compliance calendar

The closing chapter assembles everything into a single annual calendar: what recurs monthly, what recurs annually, what is event-driven, and who owns each item. It includes guidance on running the calendar with simple tools and reviewing it once a year against current regulations, since requirements change.
