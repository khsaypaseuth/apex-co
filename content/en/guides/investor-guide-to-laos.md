This guide gives foreign investors a structured orientation to investing in the Lao PDR: how the legal framework is organised, the routes into the market, what conditions and incentives look like, and the practical matters — capital, banking, visas, people — that determine whether an investment runs smoothly. It is an educational overview, not a prospectus; figures such as minimum capital, incentive rates, and tax rates change and must be confirmed at the time of investment.

> **Needs legal verification before publication.**

## Who this guide is for

- Foreign companies and individuals considering a first investment in Laos
- Investors comparing Laos with neighbouring markets
- Existing investors expanding into new activities or structures
- Advisers supporting cross-border investment into Laos

## Chapter 1 — How the Lao investment framework is organised

Laos regulates investment primarily through its investment promotion framework, administered by the investment authorities alongside the enterprise registration system. As of July 2026, this framework is the Law on Investment Promotion as amended (in force since 1 October 2024), overseen by the Investment Promotion and Management Committee (IPMC), whose office operates the central one-stop investment service. The framework divides activities into those open to general business registration and those requiring concessions — negotiated agreements with the state for land, mining, energy, and similar projects. This chapter maps the institutions an investor will deal with and how the pieces fit together.

## Chapter 2 — What you can invest in: open, conditional, and reserved activities

Not every sector is open to foreign ownership, and some are open only with conditions — ownership caps, joint venture requirements, or minimum capital levels. Lists and conditions are revised periodically. This chapter explains how to verify the current status of your intended activity before committing, and why informal workarounds such as nominee shareholders create risks that outweigh their convenience.

## Chapter 3 — Entry routes: new company, joint venture, acquisition, or concession

The main routes into the market are establishing a new wholly foreign-owned company, forming a joint venture with a Lao partner, acquiring shares in an existing company, or negotiating a concession. This chapter compares them: control, speed, dependence on partner quality, due diligence needs, and the approval steps each route involves. It gives particular attention to joint ventures — the structure many conditional sectors require — and what a well-drafted shareholder agreement should settle before money moves.

## Chapter 4 — Incentives and special economic zones

Promoted activities and promoted zones may qualify for incentives, such as tax holidays or duty exemptions, and special economic zones operate under their own regimes. Incentives are conditional: they attach to commitments about activity, location, and investment level, and can be lost if conditions are not met. This chapter explains how to assess what your project may qualify for and how to keep qualifying — without quoting rates, which must be confirmed with the investment authority.

## Chapter 5 — Capital, banking, and repatriation

Investment capital should enter Laos through proper banking channels and be documented, because that paper trail supports later profit repatriation and compliance reviews. This chapter covers the practicalities of capital registration, company banking, and the framework for remitting profits and repatriating capital, along with the currency considerations investors commonly plan around.

## Chapter 6 — People and presence: visas, work permits, land

An investment needs people on the ground and a place to operate. This chapter summarises investor visa and stay options, work permits for foreign staff and quota rules, and how premises are secured — foreigners cannot own land, so leases and concession arrangements are the norm. Each topic links to a fuller article.

## Chapter 7 — Running the investment: reporting and compliance

After approval comes the routine: periodic reporting to the investment authorities, tax filings, license renewals, and keeping registered details current. This chapter sets out the compliance rhythm and the early warning signs that a project is drifting out of compliance.
